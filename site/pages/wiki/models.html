The 3D files

The stand is two printed parts, a shell and a base. It comes in two versions: one with a USB-C port and a battery inside, and one that uses the cable that came with your display, with no soldering. Turn them around, look inside, pull them apart, and download the ones you want to print.

Drag to turn, right-drag to move, scroll or pinch to zoom. The display, the boards, the cell and the cable are simple stand-ins.

Which version

USB-C and battery puts a USB-C socket in the back wall, with an IP5306 charge board and an 18350 cell inside, so the stand runs off the desk. You cut the display’s cable and solder it to two small boards; the port still flashes the display. Display’s cable takes the MX1.25-to-USB cable that came in the box, uncut: it leaves through a slot at the bottom of the back wall and four clips in the base hold it. It runs off USB, with no battery and nothing to solder. Both use the same screws and look the same from the front.

Print one shell and the base that goes with it. Assembled, the stand is 65.5 × 91.9 × 56.5 mm. The STL files are laid out to print, on their flat bottoms, and neither needs supports. Grams are for a solid part; with walls and infill you’ll use less. Printing and assembly covers the rest.

Screws and bits

  • 4 × M3 heat-set inserts in the shell’s bosses (4.0 mm holes, up to 6 mm deep), for the base.
  • 4 × M3×8 countersunk screws up through the base into those inserts.
  • For the USB-C version: a USB-C breakout board, 22 × 13 mm with VBUS, GND, CC1, D−, D+ and CC2 pads, for the socket in the back, and for the battery an IP5306 charge and boost board (HW-775, 25 × 16 mm), an 18350 cell (protected ones fit), a flat and a spring contact plate from an 18650 holder, and a square of double-sided foam tape. Wiring is in Print the stand.

What changed in version 6

These changes come from printing version 5. The retainer ring is gone. Screwed at its front ear only, it pulled away from the display at the back and did little, and the display already holds well as a press fit in the shell. Instead, three posts on the base stand under the rim of the display’s bottom plate, one at the front and one on each side, so the display can’t be pushed down into the stand. They touch only the plain outer rim, clear of the screw heads underneath. The shell gets a pocket round each post.

Version 5’s collar was printed as a separate ring floating 0.3 mm above the rest of the shell: the lip moved up and the collar’s base didn’t. Your slicer bridged that gap, which is why the wall round the groove printed badly. The collar now grows out of the shell at full width, so the wall round the lit skirt is 2.45 mm all the way up, with no groove outside. The inside is unchanged. The two rear screw bosses, which also floated free of the shell in versions 4 and 5, now run up to the roof.

Also: 0.25 mm more room a side in the USB-C cut-out, the button hole 1.6 mm further back, a cradle 2 mm longer for protected cells, wider solder windows behind the battery contacts with buttresses behind their walls, wire clips sized for the display’s bare 3.5 mm cable, taller breakout pins to melt over, and no pocket under the IP5306’s button.

What changed in version 5

The knob turns freely. In version 4 the parts of the display that turn sat very close to the shell: the lit skirt 0.20 mm under the lip and the grip band 0.25 mm inside it, while the retainer pressed the display up against its stop with 0.25 mm of preload. A little flex, a display slightly shorter than Elecrow’s model, or the ring pulling harder at its screwed front than at its back was enough to push them into the lip, and the knob dragged so much the stand turned with it. Version 5 raises the lip 0.4 mm, opens up the bore around the skirt and the grip band, and cuts the preload to 0.10 mm. The turning parts now clear the shell by 0.55–0.6 mm, and still clear it with the display tilted 1° on its stop. The outside is unchanged. Version 5 also adds the cable version.

What changed in version 4

Version 3 took power through an inline USB-C pigtail and had an empty cradle for a cell. Version 4 makes the battery work: the back holds a USB-C breakout board on two pins, the right side holds an IP5306 charge and boost board with a hole in the wall for its button, and the wires run down the middle in the same clips. To fit the 22 mm breakout, the back of the shell is a little squarer, the plastic in front of the socket is 1 mm, and the two rear base screws moved 1 mm outwards. The retainer hasn’t changed, so a v3 retainer fits.

Changing the design

All the parts come from one parametric script, hardware/crowpanel_stand_v6.py, written with CadQuery. Every dimension is a named parameter at the top: the tilt, wall thickness, clearances, the cable run, the screw positions. Change one and run it:

pip install cadquery
DESKLING_PORT=usbc python3 crowpanel_stand_v6.py
DESKLING_PORT=cable python3 crowpanel_stand_v6.py

DESKLING_PORT picks the version. It writes STEP and STL for each part, and prints a set of checks first: that each part is one solid, the wall round the display, the display’s path in from below, both parts against the display and each other, how far the display can tilt before a turning part touches, that the posts touch only the plate’s rim, the wall around every screw hole, the cable, the boards and the cell against the walls, that the battery board’s button hole is clear, and that the cable passes the back-wall slot.

Check two numbers against your own parts first: STOP_A, the height of the display’s fixed base above its bottom plate (7.0 mm in Elecrow’s model), and CABLE2_D, the display cable’s thickness (3.5 mm assumed; the clips in both bases grip 0.05 mm under it). See Print the stand.

All the parts, and the display, share one coordinate frame (desk at z = 0, the front toward −Y), so the shell and base STEP files drop into any CAD program already in place.
