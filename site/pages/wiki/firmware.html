Inside the firmware

Three companions in one firmware

Raw, the three companions’ frames come to 22 MB, and the app partition is 14 MB, so for a while each companion was its own firmware. Now every frame’s pixels are stored deflated, the same compression as a zip file: 3.3 MB for all three, and a 5.7 MB firmware.

  • src/anims/<pet>/ holds each companion’s loops as generated C files (wisp_walk, crumb_happy…): a palette, then each frame’s pixels compressed on their own.
  • src/anims/anims.c unpacks a frame into PSRAM the first time it’s shown, with the inflater built into the ESP32-S3’s ROM. That takes a few milliseconds, well inside a frame at 24 fps; after the first time round, the loop plays straight from PSRAM. Loops stay unpacked up to 3 MB, and the one shown least recently goes first.
  • src/companion.cpp lists the companions: name, colours (screens, clock faces, the LED ring at rest, the setup page), and which loop plays which pose. A companion without a clip for something borrows another (Crumb’s Stretch is his bounce until a proper clip exists). The four need colours are shared, because they mean the same thing on every pet.
  • The dial shows the companion stored in its settings. Choosing another (on the dial, in the app, or on the firmware page) saves it and restarts, so every screen is built once for the companion it shows.

Memory: the real constraint

The ESP32-S3 has two kinds of RAM. Internal RAM is fast but small, a few hundred kilobytes, and it’s the only kind the Wi-Fi and Bluetooth drivers can use. PSRAM is 8 MB, slower, and fine for almost everything else. Most of the hard bugs came from running out of the first.

  • LVGL’s heap lives in PSRAM (a custom allocator in lv_mem_psram.c), and decoded frames go into a 2 MB PSRAM cache.
  • One small render buffer of 30 lines stays in internal RAM, because the display’s DMA needs it there.
  • Bluetooth takes about 75 KB of internal RAM; the dial’s own setup network takes about 40 KB more. They don’t fit together, so Bluetooth pauses while the setup network is up and comes back after.
  • An early version also talked to Home Assistant; dropping it freed about 40 KB, which is what made room for Bluetooth.

The setup network used to crash the dial outright: the Wi-Fi driver doesn’t fail gracefully when internal RAM runs out. The crash-report QR code on Settings > Last restart is how that was found, decoded against the build’s .elf with xtensa-esp32s3-elf-addr2line.

Animation on a microcontroller

Every loop is stored as 8-bit indexed images with a transparent palette: one 256-colour palette shared by all frames of a loop, so colours don’t flicker from frame to frame. That’s half the size of 16-bit colour, transparent (so the clock can sit behind the pet), and measured at about 40 dB against the source. The ground shadow is baked in. The bubble’s position is stored per frame so it floats with the pet’s face. Clips to dial frames covers how the frames are made.

Talking to the phone

The app and the dial speak one small JSON protocol, over HTTP on Wi-Fi (POST /api, the phone’s key in a header) or over Bluetooth (one message per line on a custom service). Pairing trades the 6-digit code on the dial’s screen for a random key. While the companion is at the dial, the dial is the source of truth: the phone sends what it logged, with the time it happened, and takes the dial’s state back.

The companion travels

The companion lives on one device at a time. It has an id, made by whichever device had it first, and a count that goes up at every move. Moving is two operations: leave hands the phone a record of the companion (who it is, which kind, the count, its care state) and freezes the dial, which remembers who has it; arrive gives a dial that record and it carries on there. Asking to leave again returns the same record, so a reply lost on a flaky Bluetooth link loses nothing. Wherever two devices disagree about where the companion is, the higher count is the newer news, and a phone tells a dial that missed some moves where the companion went since. Between phones the record travels as a link; the part after the # carries it, and browsers never send that part to the website.

For instant sync the phone keeps a Bluetooth connection open while nearby. The dial watches a counter that moves on every action and, when it changes, sends {"event":"changed"}; the phone syncs at once. Replies always carry "ok" and events never do, which is how the phone tells them apart.

Settings in flash

Settings use Arduino’s Preferences on ESP-IDF’s NVS, one key each: ssid, pass, rem_water…rem_move, h_start, h_end, tz, clock, clock_st, post, sit, stand and the rest. That’s what lets the firmware page hand a freshly flashed dial your settings: it builds the NVS partition in the browser (site/js/nvs.js) and writes it at 0x9000.
