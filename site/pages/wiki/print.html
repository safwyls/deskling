Print the stand

The stand is the only thing you make: a shell that holds the display tipped 30° toward you, a retainer ring that clamps it in from below, and a base plate that closes the bottom. In translucent filament the shell picks up the display’s LEDs as a glow around its edge.

The parts

PartSizeWhat it does
Shell65 × 91 × 53 mmThe body you see. The display goes in from below through a tilted bore and stops against a printed shoulder; a collar at the top hides everything below its black grip band. The USB-C port is in its back wall, and a small hole in its right side reaches the battery board’s button.
Retainer60 × 66 × 12 mmA ring that pushes the display’s bottom plate up against the shoulder. Two screws through its front ear hold it.
Base65 × 91 × 18 mmA 3 mm floor plate carrying the electronics: pins for the USB-C breakout at the back, a pocket for the IP5306 charge board on the right, clips for the wires down the middle, and a cradle for an 18350 cell on the left.

Put together, the stand is 65.5 × 91.1 × 56.1 mm. This is version 4 of the stand, the one with room for a battery; The 3D files says what changed.

Measure your display before you print the shell. The printed shoulder the display stops against sits 7.0 mm above the display’s bottom plate, and the lip above it at 12.3 mm. Both come from Elecrow’s 3D model, which has been wrong around the skirt before. Measure from the bottom plate to the top of the fixed base (the part that doesn’t turn) on your own display. If it isn’t 7.0 mm, change STOP_A in the script and regenerate the shell first.

Turn them around, see them pulled apart, and download STEP or STL on The 3D files.

Material

We printed the shell in translucent PETG. The display’s lit skirt, the ring of LEDs that turns with the knob, sits inside the shell just under its collar, so a clear or frosted filament carries its colour out around the display’s edge: blue when water is due, amber when it’s time for an eye break. Opaque filament works fine too; you just lose the glow. The retainer and base are hidden, so print them in whatever you have.

Settings to start from

These are sensible defaults for functional PETG parts, not a tuned profile. Adjust for your printer.

SupportsOnly where notedThe shell needs one painted-on support: the seat inside the front slot that the retainer’s ear clamps against, a flat ceiling about 20 × 9.5 mm tilted 30°. The support comes out through the bottom opening. The rest of the shell prints without. The retainer’s front is trimmed flat to sit on the base, so with the flange down it rises from the bed at 30°, up to about 5.3 mm: turn on build-plate-only supports for it. Don’t flip it onto the flat cut face instead; the whole ring then prints tilted, with far more steep overhangs. The base needs none.

Putting it together

  1. Inserts. Melt four M3 heat-set inserts into the bosses on the shell’s underside, two at the front and two at the back (4.0 mm holes, up to 6 mm deep).
  2. Display. Push the display up the bore from underneath, screen first, until its fixed base stops against the printed shoulder. Above it, the lip and collar cover the lit skirt, the gold groove and the notch, so only the black grip band shows; the turning parts clear the lip by about 0.2 mm.
  3. Retainer. Slide the retainer up behind the display, its front ear up the slot in the front of the shell, and drive two M3×8 socket-head screws up through the ear into the pilot holes (they bite about 5 mm into the 6.5 mm pilots; longer ones bottom out). The ring stands 0.25 mm proud, so tightening the screws preloads the display against the stop and it doesn’t rattle; its flange keeps the back pressed down too.
  4. Electronics. Wire the boards as in Battery and charging below, before they go in. Then press the USB-C breakout onto the two pins at the back of the base, its socket facing backwards; a touch of a hot soldering iron on the pin tips, or a drop of glue, keeps it there. Glue the IP5306 board into its pocket on the right, button facing the right-hand wall. Snap the cell into the cradle, press the wires into the clips down the middle, and lead the display’s pair up through the hole in the floor.
  5. Base. Close it up with four M3×8 countersunk screws from below, into the inserts. The 3 mm base lets the heads sit flush.

Battery and charging

The display itself only runs on 5 V and has no battery input, so the cell needs a board that charges it and boosts it to 5 V. The stand is designed around two cheap modules:

PartWhat it does
USB-C breakout, 22 × 13 mm, pads marked VBUS, GND, CC1, D−, D+, CC2The socket in the stand’s back wall, for charging and for flashing. It already has the two 5.1 kΩ resistors (marked 512) that make a USB-C charger supply power.
The MX1.25-to-USB cable that comes with the displayIts small 4-pin plug fits the display’s USB connector (J37 on Elecrow’s schematic): 5 V in, D−, D+ and ground, wired straight to the ESP32-S3’s own USB pins. Cut off the USB end and it becomes the link from the stand’s socket to the display, power and data both, with no soldering on the display.
IP5306 charge and boost board (HW-775), 25 × 16 mm, pads OUT-5V, GND, BAT, GND, GND, VINCharges the cell from USB, turns it into 5 V for the display, and keeps the display running while it charges. Its four LEDs show the charge level and glow through the right side of the shell.
18350 Li-ion cell, and a flat and a spring contact plate from a standard 18650 holderThe battery. A protected cell is the safer choice.
Thin wireSilicone-insulated, 26–28 AWG is plenty.

Wire it like this:

FromTo
Breakout VBUSIP5306 VIN
Breakout GNDIP5306 GND, the pad next to VIN
IP5306 BATThe flat contact at the front of the cradle (the cell’s +)
IP5306 GNDThe spring contact at the back of the cradle (the cell’s −)
IP5306 OUT-5VThe display cable’s 5 V wire (pin 1 at the plug)
IP5306 GNDThe display cable’s ground wire (pin 4)
Breakout D− and D+The display cable’s D− (pin 2) and D+ (pin 3). Twist the two together; at the ESP32’s 12 Mbit/s, short plain wires are fine.
Breakout CC1, CC2Nothing. The resistors they need are already on the board.

Plugging in a USB-C charger switches the board on and starts charging. Running on the battery, a short press of its button (through the hole in the right side, with a pen tip) switches it on, and on most IP5306 boards two quick presses switch it off. The board also switches itself off if the load stays very light for about half a minute, so if the dial goes dark during a long nap, that’s why.

How long a charge lasts depends on how much the display draws, which we haven’t measured yet. Expect a few hours rather than days: an 18350 holds about 4 Wh, and the dial with its screen and Bluetooth on probably uses somewhere around half a watt to a watt.

The colours inside the display cable aren’t standardised, so check which wire is which with a multimeter against the pins of its plug before you solder. Plug it into the display with the cable led down through the hole in the stand’s floor.

With the data pair wired, a computer on the stand’s USB-C port reaches the ESP32 directly, so the firmware page can flash a closed-up stand. The IP5306 keeps the 5 V coming while it does; the computer only carries data.

No battery? Leave out the IP5306 and the cell, and wire the breakout’s VBUS, D−, D+ and GND straight to the display cable’s four wires. Everything else is the same.

Check your boards against the model. The sizes come from the sellers’ listings, which are only good to a millimetre or two. Before printing, check the breakout’s hole spacing (16.3 mm in the model) and how far the IP5306’s button sits from its end (3.5 mm), and change BRK_HOLE_X or KEY_IN in the script if yours differ.

Do the first flash before you close the base anyway. The ESP32-S3 normally switches into its download mode over USB by itself, but if it ever doesn’t, the fix is holding its BOOT button and tapping RESET, and those are on the display’s underside, under the base.
