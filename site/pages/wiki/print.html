Print the stand

The stand is the only thing you make: a shell that holds the display tipped 30° toward you, and a base plate that closes the bottom and stops the display being pushed in. In translucent filament the shell picks up the display’s LEDs as a glow around its edge.

Pick a version

There are three versions. They look the same from the front.

Put together, the stand is 65.5 × 91.9 × 56.5 mm, or 81.5 mm tall on the tray. This is version 7 of the stand; The 3D files says what changed.

Measure your display before you print. The printed shoulder the display stops against sits 7.0 mm above the display’s bottom plate, and the base’s posts reach up to that plate. Both come from Elecrow’s 3D model, which has been wrong around the skirt before. Measure from the bottom plate to the top of the fixed base (the part that doesn’t turn) on your own display. If it isn’t 7.0 mm, change STOP_A in the script and regenerate both parts. The clips are drawn for the display’s cable at 4.0 mm across (CABLE2_D); check yours.

Turn them around, see them pulled apart, and download STEP or STL on The 3D files.

Material

We printed the shell in translucent PETG. The display’s lit skirt, the ring of LEDs that turns with the knob, sits inside the shell just under its collar, so a clear or frosted filament carries its colour out around the display’s edge: blue when water is due, amber when it’s time for an eye break. Opaque filament works fine too; you just lose the glow. The base and tray don’t need to glow, so print them in whatever you have.

Settings to start from

These are sensible defaults for functional PETG parts, not a tuned profile. Adjust for your printer.

Putting it together

  1. Inserts. Melt four M3 heat-set inserts into the bosses on the shell’s underside, two at the front and two at the back (4.0 mm holes, up to 6 mm deep).
  2. Display. Push the display up the bore from underneath, screen first, until its fixed base stops against the printed shoulder. It’s a press fit, so it stays put on its own. Above it, the lip and collar cover the lit skirt, the gold groove and the notch, so only the black grip band shows; the turning parts clear the shell by about 0.6 mm. Turn the knob: it should turn as freely as it does out of the stand.
  3. Electronics, USB-C versions. Wire the breakout to the display’s cable as in Wiring the USB-C versions below. Press the breakout onto the two pins at the back of the base, its socket facing backwards, and melt the pin tops over the holes with a soldering iron so it can’t lift. Press the cable into the clips down the middle and lead its plug up through the hole in the floor, then plug it into the display.
  4. Battery, 18650 version. Wire the IP5306 and the cell contacts too, leading the board’s four wires down through the slot in the deck. Stick the IP5306 between its rails in the tray with a square of double-sided foam tape, button facing the window. Push the contact plates into their slots at the ends of the cradle, flat one at the front, and snap the cell in.
  5. Cable, cable version. Plug the cable’s small 4-pin plug into the display’s USB connector, lead it down through the hole in the floor, then lay it along the middle of the base and press it into the four clips, the last one just inside the back wall. Set the shell onto the base with the cable in the slot at the bottom of the back wall; the base closes the slot underneath.
  6. Close it. Four M3×8 countersunk screws go up through the base into the inserts; for the 18650 version, four M3×30 socket-head screws go up through the tray’s columns and the deck instead. As the base comes up, its three posts slide into pockets in the shell and finish under the rim of the display’s bottom plate. If it won’t pull flat, the posts are pushing the display up: your display’s fixed base is taller than 7.0 mm. Take a little off the post tops, or set STOP_GAP in the script.

Wiring the USB-C versions

Without a battery it’s four wires: the breakout’s VBUS, D−, D+ and GND go straight to the display cable’s red, white, green and black wires, once you’ve cut its USB end off. Leave CC1 and CC2 unconnected.

With the 18650, the display runs on 5 V from the cell. Elecrow’s schematic has a battery connector and charger on its board, but retail boards come with those parts left off (only their footprints are there), so the stand brings its own: a board that charges the cell and boosts it to 5 V. It’s designed around these parts:

PartWhat it does
USB-C breakout, 22 × 13 mm, pads marked VBUS, GND, CC1, D−, D+, CC2The socket in the stand’s back wall, for charging and for flashing. It already has the two 5.1 kΩ resistors (marked 512) that make a USB-C charger supply power.
The MX1.25-to-USB cable that comes with the displayIts small 4-pin plug fits the display’s USB connector (J37 on Elecrow’s schematic): 5 V in, D−, D+ and ground, wired straight to the ESP32-S3’s own USB pins. Cut off the USB end and it becomes the link from the stand’s socket to the display, power and data both, with no soldering on the display.
IP5306 charge and boost board (HW-775), 25 × 16 mm, pads OUT-5V, GND, BAT, GND, GND, VINCharges the cell from USB, turns it into 5 V for the display, and keeps the display running while it charges. Its four LEDs show the charge level through the window in the tray’s right side.
18650 Li-ion cell, and a flat and a spring contact plate from a standard 18650 holderThe battery. The IP5306 charges at up to about 2 A and can’t be turned down, so pick a cell whose datasheet allows a 2 A charge; most 2500–3500 mAh cells do. A protected cell is the safer choice; ones up to 69 mm long fit.
Thin wireSilicone-insulated, 26–28 AWG is plenty.

IP5306 GNDThe display cable’s black wire, ground (pin 4)

Breakout CC1, CC2Nothing. The resistors they need are already on the board.

Plugging in a USB-C charger switches the board on and starts charging. Running on the battery, a short press of its button (through the window in the tray’s right side) switches it on, and on most IP5306 boards two quick presses switch it off. The board also switches itself off if the load stays under about 45 mA for about half a minute, which a napping dial with its screen dimmed and its ring off draws less than. From firmware 5.21 the dial keeps it awake: while the screen is dimmed, it keeps its Wi-Fi radio fully on and adds a burst of processor load every few seconds. Nothing shows, but napping then drains the battery about as fast as being awake. The dial needs to be on Wi-Fi for this; without Wi-Fi the bursts alone may not be enough.

How long a charge lasts depends on how much the display draws, which we haven’t measured yet. A 2500–3500 mAh 18650 holds about 9–12 Wh, and the dial with its screen and Bluetooth on probably uses somewhere around half a watt to a watt, so expect something like half a day to a day.

The display cable uses the standard USB colours: red 5 V, white D−, green D+, black ground. Cheap cables occasionally swap them, so check red against pin 1 of the plug with a multimeter before you solder: 5 V on a data wire goes straight into the ESP32’s USB pins, which aren’t 5 V tolerant. If there’s a bare drain wire or foil shield, trim it back. Plug it into the display with the cable led down through the hole in the stand’s floor.

With the data pair wired, a computer on the stand’s USB-C port reaches the ESP32 directly, so the firmware page can flash a closed-up stand. The IP5306 keeps the 5 V coming while it does; the computer only carries data.

Check your boards against the model. The sizes come from the sellers’ listings, which are only good to a millimetre or two. Before printing, check the breakout’s hole spacing (16.3 mm in the model) and how far the IP5306’s button sits from its end (5.1 mm), and change BRK_HOLE_X or KEY_IN in the script if yours differ.

Do the first flash before you close the stand anyway, whichever version you built. The ESP32-S3 normally switches into its download mode over USB by itself, but if it ever doesn’t, the fix is holding its BOOT button and tapping RESET, and those are on the display’s underside, under the base.
