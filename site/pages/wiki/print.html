Print the stand

The stand is the only thing you make: a shell that holds the display tipped 30° toward you, and a base plate that closes the bottom and stops the display being pushed in. In translucent filament the shell picks up the display’s LEDs as a glow around its edge.

Pick a version

There are two versions of the shell and base. They look the same from the front and take the same screws.

VersionWhat you needWhat you get
USB-C and batteryA USB-C breakout, an IP5306 charge board, an 18350 cell, and some soldering. The display’s cable gets cut.A USB-C port in the back that charges, powers and flashes the display, and a few hours away from the cable.
Display’s cableNothing beyond the printed parts and screws: the MX1.25-to-USB cable that came with the display goes in whole.The cable leaves through a slot at the bottom of the back wall. Runs and flashes over that cable; no battery.

The parts

PartSizeWhat it does
Shell65 × 92 × 54 mmThe body you see. The display presses in from below through a tilted bore and stops against a printed shoulder; a collar at the top hides everything below its black grip band. The USB-C version has the port in its back wall and a small hole in its right side for the battery board’s button; the cable version has a slot for the cable instead.
Base65 × 91 × 19 mmA 3 mm floor plate with three posts that stand under the rim of the display’s bottom plate, so it can’t be pushed down. The USB-C base also carries the electronics: pins for the breakout at the back, rails for the IP5306 board on the right, clips for the cable down the middle, and a cradle for an 18350 cell on the left. The cable base has four clips that grip the cable.

For the USB-C version there’s also a shell with a charge window: one opening low in the right side that shows the IP5306 board’s four charge LEDs and its button. It takes the same base. Get it from The 3D files.

Put together, the stand is 65.5 × 91.9 × 56.5 mm. This is version 6 of the stand; The 3D files says what changed.

Measure your display before you print. The printed shoulder the display stops against sits 7.0 mm above the display’s bottom plate, and the base’s posts reach up to that plate. Both come from Elecrow’s 3D model, which has been wrong around the skirt before. Measure from the bottom plate to the top of the fixed base (the part that doesn’t turn) on your own display. If it isn’t 7.0 mm, change STOP_A in the script and regenerate both parts. The clips are drawn for the display’s cable at 4.0 mm across (CABLE2_D); check yours.

Turn them around, see them pulled apart, and download STEP or STL on The 3D files.

Material

We printed the shell in translucent PETG. The display’s lit skirt, the ring of LEDs that turns with the knob, sits inside the shell just under its collar, so a clear or frosted filament carries its colour out around the display’s edge: blue when water is due, amber when it’s time for an eye break. Opaque filament works fine too; you just lose the glow. The base is hidden, so print it in whatever you have.

Putting it together

  1. Inserts. Melt four M3 heat-set inserts into the bosses on the shell’s underside, two at the front and two at the back (4.0 mm holes, up to 6 mm deep).
  2. Display. Push the display up the bore from underneath, screen first, until its fixed base stops against the printed shoulder. It’s a press fit, so it stays put on its own. Above it, the lip and collar cover the lit skirt, the gold groove and the notch, so only the black grip band shows; the turning parts clear the shell by about 0.6 mm. Turn the knob: it should turn as freely as it does out of the stand.
  3. Electronics, USB-C version. Wire the boards as in Battery and charging below, before they go in. Then press the USB-C breakout onto the two pins at the back of the base, its socket facing backwards, and melt the pin tops over the holes with a soldering iron so it can’t lift. Stick the IP5306 board between its rails on the right with a square of double-sided foam tape, button facing the right-hand wall. Snap the cell into the cradle, press the cable into the clips down the middle, and lead its plug up through the hole in the floor.
  4. Cable, cable version. Plug the cable’s small 4-pin plug into the display’s USB connector, lead it down through the hole in the floor, then lay it along the middle of the base and press it into the four clips, the last one just inside the back wall. Set the shell onto the base with the cable in the slot at the bottom of the back wall; the base closes the slot underneath.
  5. Base. Close it up with four M3×8 countersunk screws from below, into the inserts. As the base comes up, its three posts slide into pockets in the shell and finish under the rim of the display’s bottom plate. If the base won’t pull flat, the posts are pushing the display up: your display’s fixed base is taller than 7.0 mm. Take a little off the post tops, or set STOP_GAP in the script.

Battery and charging

This part is for the USB-C version. The display runs on 5 V. Elecrow’s schematic has a battery connector and charger on its board, but retail boards come with those parts left off (only their footprints are there), so the stand brings its own: a board that charges the cell and boosts it to 5 V. It’s designed around two cheap modules:

PartWhat it does
USB-C breakout, 22 × 13 mm, pads marked VBUS, GND, CC1, D−, D+, CC2The socket in the stand’s back wall, for charging and for flashing. It already has the two 5.1 kΩ resistors (marked 512) that make a USB-C charger supply power.
The MX1.25-to-USB cable that comes with the displayIts small 4-pin plug fits the display’s USB connector (J37 on Elecrow’s schematic): 5 V in, D−, D+ and ground, wired straight to the ESP32-S3’s own USB pins. Cut off the USB end and it becomes the link from the stand’s socket to the display, power and data both, with no soldering on the display.
IP5306 charge and boost board (HW-775), 25 × 16 mm, pads OUT-5V, GND, BAT, GND, GND, VINCharges the cell from USB, turns it into 5 V for the display, and keeps the display running while it charges. Its four LEDs show the charge level and glow through the right side of the shell.
18350 Li-ion cell, and a flat and a spring contact plate from a standard 18650 holderThe battery. A protected cell is the safer choice.
Thin wireSilicone-insulated, 26–28 AWG is plenty.

Wire it like this:

FromTo
Breakout VBUSIP5306 VIN
Breakout GNDIP5306 GND, the pad next to VIN
IP5306 BATThe flat contact at the front of the cradle (the cell’s +)
IP5306 GNDThe spring contact at the back of the cradle (the cell’s −)
IP5306 OUT-5VThe display cable’s red wire, 5 V (pin 1 at the plug)
IP5306 GNDThe display cable’s black wire, ground (pin 4)
Breakout D− and D+The display cable’s white wire, D− (pin 2), and green wire, D+ (pin 3). Twist the two together; at the ESP32’s 12 Mbit/s, short plain wires are fine.
Breakout CC1, CC2Nothing. The resistors they need are already on the board.

Plugging in a USB-C charger switches the board on and starts charging. Running on the battery, a short press of its button (through the hole in the right side, with a pen tip) switches it on, and on most IP5306 boards two quick presses switch it off. The board also switches itself off if the load stays under about 45 mA for about half a minute, which a napping dial with its screen dimmed and its ring off draws less than. From firmware 5.21 the dial keeps it awake: while the screen is dimmed, it keeps its Wi-Fi radio fully on and adds a burst of processor load every few seconds. Nothing shows, but napping then drains the battery about as fast as being awake. The dial needs to be on Wi-Fi for this; without Wi-Fi the bursts alone may not be enough.

How long a charge lasts depends on how much the display draws, which we haven’t measured yet. Expect a few hours rather than days: an 18350 holds about 4 Wh, and the dial with its screen and Bluetooth on probably uses somewhere around half a watt to a watt.

The display cable uses the standard USB colours: red 5 V, white D−, green D+, black ground. Cheap cables occasionally swap them, so check red against pin 1 of the plug with a multimeter before you solder: 5 V on a data wire goes straight into the ESP32’s USB pins, which aren’t 5 V tolerant. If there’s a bare drain wire or foil shield, trim it back. Plug it into the display with the cable led down through the hole in the stand’s floor.

With the data pair wired, a computer on the stand’s USB-C port reaches the ESP32 directly, so the firmware page can flash a closed-up stand. The IP5306 keeps the 5 V coming while it does; the computer only carries data.

No battery? Leave out the IP5306 and the cell, and wire the breakout’s VBUS, D−, D+ and GND straight to the display cable’s red, white, green and black wires. Everything else is the same.

Check your boards against the model. The sizes come from the sellers’ listings, which are only good to a millimetre or two. Before printing, check the breakout’s hole spacing (16.3 mm in the model) and how far the IP5306’s button sits from its end (5.1 mm), and change BRK_HOLE_X or KEY_IN in the script if yours differ.

Do the first flash before you close the base anyway, whichever version you built. The ESP32-S3 normally switches into its download mode over USB by itself, but if it ever doesn’t, the fix is holding its BOOT button and tapping RESET, and those are on the display’s underside, under the base.
