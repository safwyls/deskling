Print the stand

The stand is the only thing you make: a shell that holds the display tipped 30° toward you, and a deck that closes the bottom and back and carries a USB-C port. In translucent filament the shell picks up the display’s LEDs as a glow around its edge.

The parts

PartSizeWhat it does
Shell65 × 65 × 54 mmThe body you see. The display presses in from below through a tilted bore and stops against a printed shoulder; a collar at the top hides everything below its black grip band.
Deck65 × 64 × 42 mmThe floor and the curved back wall in one piece. The back wall has the USB-C port; the floor has pins for the USB-C breakout, clips for the cable, and three posts that stand under the rim of the display’s bottom plate so it can’t be pushed down. Two tall posts locate it in the shell.

Put together, the stand is 65 × 65 × 56.5 mm. This is version 8 of the stand; The 3D files has the files to download, a 3D view, and what changed.

Measure your display before you print. The printed shoulder the display stops against sits 7.0 mm above the display’s bottom plate, and the deck’s posts reach up to that plate. Both come from Elecrow’s 3D model, which has been wrong around the skirt before. Measure from the bottom plate to the top of the fixed base (the part that doesn’t turn) on your own display. If it isn’t 7.0 mm, the shoulder and posts need moving to match before you print.

Material

We printed the shell in translucent PETG. The display’s lit skirt, the ring of LEDs that turns with the knob, sits inside the shell just under its collar, so a clear or frosted filament carries its colour out around the display’s edge: blue when water is due, amber when it’s time for an eye break. Opaque filament works fine too; you just lose the glow. The deck is mostly hidden, so print it in whatever you have.

Putting it together

  1. Inserts. Melt four M3 heat-set inserts into the bosses on the shell’s underside, two at the front and one on each side (4.0 mm holes, 6 mm deep).
  2. Display. Push the display up the bore from underneath, screen first, until its fixed base stops against the printed shoulder. It’s a press fit, so it stays put on its own. Above it, the lip and collar cover the lit skirt, the gold groove and the notch, so only the black grip band shows. Turn the knob: it should turn as freely as it does out of the stand.
  3. Wiring. Wire the breakout to the display’s cable as below. Press the breakout onto the two pins at the back of the deck, its socket in the port in the back wall, and melt the pin tops over the holes with a soldering iron so it can’t lift. Press the cable into the clips and plug it into the display.
  4. Close it. Slide the deck up into the shell, its two tall posts into the sockets inside, and fix it with four M3×8 countersunk screws from below into the inserts. As the deck comes up, its three short posts finish under the rim of the display’s bottom plate. If it won’t pull flat, the posts are pushing the display up: your display’s fixed base is taller than 7.0 mm. Take a little off the post tops.

Wiring

The stand’s USB-C port is a small breakout board wired to the cable that came with the display.

PartWhat it does
USB-C breakout, 22 × 13 mm, pads marked VBUS, GND, CC1, D−, D+, CC2The socket in the stand’s back wall, for power and for flashing. It already has the two 5.1 kΩ resistors (marked 512) that make a USB-C charger supply power.
The MX1.25-to-USB cable that comes with the displayIts small 4-pin plug fits the display’s USB connector (J37 on Elecrow’s schematic): 5 V in, D−, D+ and ground, wired straight to the ESP32-S3’s own USB pins. Cut off the USB end and it becomes the link from the stand’s socket to the display, power and data both, with no soldering on the display.
BreakoutDisplay cable
VBUSRed, 5 V (pin 1 at the plug)
D−White (pin 2)
D+Green (pin 3)
GNDBlack, ground (pin 4)
CC1, CC2Nothing. The resistors they need are already on the board.

Twist the white and green wires together; at the ESP32’s 12 Mbit/s, short plain wires are fine. The cable uses the standard USB colours, but cheap cables occasionally swap them, so check red against pin 1 of the plug with a multimeter before you solder: 5 V on a data wire goes straight into the ESP32’s USB pins, which aren’t 5 V tolerant. If there’s a bare drain wire or foil shield, trim it back.

With the data pair wired, a computer on the stand’s USB-C port reaches the ESP32 directly, so the firmware page can flash a closed-up stand.

Rather not solder? The cable version of the stand keeps the display’s cable whole and leads it out of the back.

Check your breakout against the model. The size comes from the seller’s listing, which is only good to a millimetre or two. Before printing, check that its two holes are 16.4 mm apart, centre to centre, to match the deck’s pins.

Do the first flash before you close the stand anyway, whichever version you built. The ESP32-S3 normally switches into its download mode over USB by itself, but if it ever doesn’t, the fix is holding its BOOT button and tapping RESET, and those are on the display’s underside, under the base.
