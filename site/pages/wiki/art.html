Deep dive

From sketches to characters

Every frame your companion plays started as a still image from ChatGPT and became motion in a video model. Here’s the path, including the detour that didn’t work.

1. Five candidates, ten poses each

It started with five characters: Mochi, Sprig, Cinder, Crumb and Wisp. Each got a short personality brief and pose directions written for its body. Wisp glides instead of walking; Crumb hides in his shell to sleep; Sprig’s leaves give its mood away. Then came one action and emotion study per character, made with ChatGPT’s image generation from that character’s original drawing.

The prompt did a lot of the design work. Some of the rules that mattered:

  • Use the reference strictly for identity and style. The original drawing pins down the character; the sheet only adds poses.
  • Ten equal round navy vignettes in a 5 × 2 grid, each representing the 360×360 screen, with the whole character and any action cue inside its circle. Composing for the real screen from the first sketch saved trouble later.
  • Exactly these labels, in this order: walking, running, jumping, sleeping, happy / sad, mad, tired, thirsty, restless.
  • Distinctions the eye needs at 1.46 inches: asleep is curled and low, tired is awake but sagging, restless is fidgety but not angry, and mad must not look like sad.
  • Thirst gets an empty bowl and a droplet, so it can’t be mistaken for sadness.

Wisp, Crumb and Sprig made it onto the dial. Mochi and Cinder are still sketches, waiting for someone to animate them.

2. The detour: a cut-out puppet

The first plan was to animate in code: split each character into parts (body, fins, feet, crest, a set of face masks) and move the pieces like a paper puppet. ChatGPT produced the part sheets, and a script cleaned them up and lined them up on a shared scale.

It worked, but it looked like a puppet. Pieces rotating around pins don’t blend the way drawn animation does, and a squash, a wince or a yawn needs the whole silhouette to change. That’s the moment the project switched to video.

3. One reference per pose

Next, each pose got its own reference image, made with ChatGPT from the study sheet: one clear pose per image, in its navy circle. Those are the starting frames for the video model. Starting from a single, clean pose keeps the character on model through the clip.

4. Video from each reference

The references went into Scenario, using its image-to-video mode with MiniMax H3, one 5-second clip per pose. Three things in the prompt made the clips usable:

  • In place. The character acts out the pose on the spot, without walking across the frame, so the clip can loop on a small screen.
  • A flat magenta background (pure #FF00FF), a colour none of the characters use, so it can be keyed out cleanly.
  • A locked camera. No zooms or pans, so every frame lines up with the last.

What still goes wrong

Video models don’t always listen. Some clips that were thrown out or trimmed:

  • Crumb’s restless clip walked out of frame, and its background turned navy halfway through.
  • Sprig’s restless clip flickered between grey and magenta backgrounds while Sprig spun around.
  • Crumb’s mad clip pushed the camera in after a second and a half, so only the first part is used.
  • Backgrounds drift between a hot and a dull magenta from clip to clip. The keying tool copes with that, as long as the colour stays in the magenta family.

Generate a few and keep the best. A pet with a missing pose isn’t blocked: a stand-in loop covers it until a good clip turns up.
